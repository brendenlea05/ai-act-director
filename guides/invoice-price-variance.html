Purchasing controls / pricing

Checking vendor invoice prices against the purchase order

A price difference is easiest to resolve when someone can point to the exact purchase-order line, invoiced rate and agreed unit of measure. A report saying “invoice is wrong” isn't nearly as useful as one showing which SKU needs review.

What counts as a price variance?

Imagine an order for 100 units at $12.50 each and an invoice billing those same 100 units at $13.00. The nominal difference is 50 cents per unit, or $50 in total. That deserves review, but it may reflect an authorized change, a freight-inclusive quote or a later credit. A variance is a starting point for a question, not a determination of debt.

How to check the calculation

  1. Find the purchase order and match the supplier SKU to the invoiced item. Check the PO version and any approved change orders.
  2. Confirm that both prices use the same units. If the PO is per case and the invoice is per each, convert before comparing.
  3. Make sure the currency matches and that taxes, freight and fees are not being mistaken for product unit prices.
  4. Choose a sensible rounding tolerance. Small differences can be caused by export precision rather than a meaningful price change.
  5. Confirm the quantity to which the disputed price applies. Partial shipments and multiple price tiers often require more context.

What the CSV checker can do

For orders with multiple negotiated price tiers, a weighted average may not be the right commercial interpretation; verify the original agreement.

Once you've verified the difference, record the PO, SKU, invoice identifier, prices and the agreement you relied on. A neutral vendor question is more productive than an accusation.

Frequently asked question

Is a price variance always an overcharge?

No. Verify amendments to the PO, discounts, taxes, freight, partial shipments, credits and supplier contracts before challenging a bill.
